## The Story Behind Scale

**Host:** All right, [music] full rock-star treatment for Alexandr Wang, everyone. [cheering] [cheering] [cheering] [applause]

Backstage, we were saying that one of the cool ways to think about this event is that this room is full of people who are just like us when we were 18 or 20. There are even some 16-year-olds in this audience.

Let's jump to your story. You came up really smart, with math olympiads and computer science competitions. Take us to the Alex of that time. What were you feeling? What were you thinking? What drove you down this road?

**Alexandr Wang:** I grew up in Los Alamos, New Mexico, which is now famous because of *Oppenheimer*, but it really was the middle of nowhere. I did all these math competitions and computer science competitions, but I knew I wanted to do really big things. It wasn't exactly clear how, or what the exact path to do that would be.

I had a friend who was really into programming. After high school, he got an internship in the Valley. I think his first internship was at Palantir. He was an influence for me, so after I finished high school, I ended up working at Quora here in Silicon Valley.

I worked there for a year. I took a gap year to work there, and then I went to MIT. I was 19 when I worked at Quora, 18 when I went to MIT, and 19 when I started Scale.

I remember that period from 17 to 19. I felt like I was constantly changing. Exactly what I wanted to do was constantly changing. I was learning so much just from the people around me. It felt like I was drinking from a fire hose pretty constantly during that time.

I would definitely recommend two things that were really important. One is that working at a company was really valuable, because from the outside in, you have no idea how companies work. You have no idea what it looks like to actually build something, iterate on something, or see groups of people make decisions.

Going to school at MIT was also really important, because it gave me a lot of opportunity to explore what was interesting. It was at MIT that I started training my first models, played around with TensorFlow, which had just come out that year, and ultimately came up with the idea for Scale.

After one year at MIT, I applied to YC. It felt like a miracle to get in at that time. YC was really critical to my entrepreneurial journey. I don't think YC is just supportive and wants you to succeed; they also give it to you very real. They tell you when you're being a dumbass, which is what we all need in life.

That was the story up until then. I was 19 when I started Scale, and the rest is history.

**Host:** You worked with Jared Friedman at the time, and you came in with a very different idea from what ended up becoming Scale.

**Alexandr Wang:** We wanted to build an AI agent, funnily enough, to help people get medical care. It was the right kind of idea—an idea that I think will ultimately exist. We're even seeing it now. AI agents to help people get medical care are very real.

But it was the wrong timing. We worked on it for about a month or two before Jared pulled us aside and said, “Guys, I don't know if this is going to go anywhere.” [laughter]

That's exactly what we needed to hear. At that time, I had studied AI at MIT and trained models. We went back to the drawing board, thought deeply about where the opportunity was, and came up with Scale.

**Host:** You were selling data at a time when large language models had not really come to the fore yet. Self-driving cars were coming up, and computer vision suddenly became important. Was that the first market?

**Alexandr Wang:** When I was at MIT, I did a bunch of projects and trained models of various forms. By comparison with today, they were little toy models.

To train a model, I needed three things. I needed a GCP account—an account on some cloud service to get compute. I needed the code to run to actually train the model. And I needed data. I needed a data set.

For two out of those three things, you could just press a button online and get them. For the last one, data, there was no effective way to get data for training these models. It felt incredibly obvious that there was going to be a way to press a button, so to speak, and get data.

It was funny, because in the first many years of Scale, data was still very unsexy. Every time we went out to fundraise, even though our numbers were great and we had great revenue, VCs and investors were always skeptical. They would say, “I don't know if this is a good business. Does it have longevity? Is it durable?”

It was weird to me, but none of the investors had ever trained a model, so I guess they didn't really get it. Fast-forward to today: we managed to raise money, keep going, and keep growing the business. The very same investors who passed on us and were dour on the potential of AI are writing think pieces about how data is so critical and is one of the biggest business opportunities in AI.

It's funny to see the whole thing come full circle.

## First-Principles Thinking

**Host:** That seems like a good case study in first-principles thinking. You can't start a company by opening the pages of the *Wall Street Journal* and saying, “This data is hot. We're going to go work on that.” You literally couldn't have started Scale that way. You had to start with simple statements about the world that you knew to be true, and then build something for that.

**Alexandr Wang:** The key thing is that you need to develop conviction in a set of beliefs that nobody else agrees with.

If you look at all the most successful companies in the world, they were started long before the core idea was popular. They toiled in obscurity for years and years before the idea, space, or concept of the business became consensus. The only way you're going to be successful is if you're able to identify these truths about the world early, long before everyone else.

One of the most surprising things is that Scale has been working on AI for a decade. You can't base your business decisions on what everyone else is saying around you. If you go too much with the herd, you will get immensely confused and end up nowhere.

You have to develop your own compass for what you think the future is going to look like, because everyone else will just confuse you.

**Host:** One of the things you got incredibly great at was starting with this kernel—“We believe X, and nobody else believes it”—and then dealing with the mechanics of building the business: talking to investors and convincing them, not letting them demoralize you, talking to customers who should get it, and convincing people to come work for you.

**Alexandr Wang:** The early mechanics of building a company are things you might have some predisposition to be good at, but nobody is good at starting a company when they start one. I remember talking to a lot of the investors I met very early on. They would say, “You just grew so quickly and changed so quickly.” I didn't see it at the time.

I think that's probably true for literally everyone who starts a company. Nobody is good at something they've never done before. The whole game is: how do you develop yourself to continuously improve, get better, and learn quickly?

## A Once-in-a-Civilization Opportunity

**Host:** Backstage, we were talking about how this is a lucky time to start a company. You can come to YC, and the people in this room have each other, which is wild. But now you also have an ideal personal AI that can tell you, “Here are some ways to do it.”

Would that have helped you accelerate even faster? What do you think it's like to start a company today, in the age of AI?

**Alexandr Wang:** I really think we're at this amazing moment in the world where the bottleneck is not the progress of the AI models. The bottleneck is diffusing that through the rest of the world and helping the world adapt to this amazing technology that already exists.

If the models didn't improve at all from today, there would still be decades and decades of total upheaval and change in the economy, in how the world operates, and in everything around us.

As a result, it's probably a once-in-a-civilization opportunity to be a dreamer, have a vision, have ambition, and impose a view of how the future world should look by building something amazing.

When I started Scale ten years ago, if you started a company, it was David versus Goliath. You had to be clever, find an angle into the market, and figure out a way to compete even though you had much fewer resources.

Now, with the power of agents and AI broadly speaking, I think it's much closer to Goliath versus Goliath. Maybe the startup is a megagoliath, vastly enhanced by the power of agents and AI, while the large companies are the more traditional Goliath.

If startups properly embrace AI agents and figure out how to leverage their strengths in the most ambitious ways, they can easily outcompete incumbents.

## Personal Superintelligence

**Host:** Let's talk about superintelligence, because that's clearly even in the name of your lab. What does superintelligence mean operationally inside Meta right now?

**Alexandr Wang:** A year ago, Mark wrote a memo about personal superintelligence, which I think is very similar to your concept of personal AGI.

We believe that everybody in the world—all the billions of people in the world—are going to have a superintelligence adapted and tailored to them. It will enable them to accomplish their goals, know their context, and ultimately be an expander of their own agency.

The thing we think a lot about is agency expansion. How do we help people accomplish things they couldn't have ever dreamed of before? What would everyone in the world do if everything were just easy?

We think about this in an ecosystem way as well. We don't believe in a totalizing, totalitarian view of AIs that control the world. We believe these systems are going to enhance a very broad ecosystem.

We believe in billions of people all around the world having their own personal superintelligence. We also believe in an explosion of entrepreneurship. There are 200 million businesses on Meta's platforms today. We think that number should go to billions with this explosion of creativity and the use of AI tools.

Ultimately, we think it will be a dynamic ecosystem of business agents working with personal agents and developing a complex ecosystem that is fully AI-supercharged.

## Building a Frontier Lab

**Host:** I was really psyched to see Meta Muse Spark 1.1. My OpenClaw absolutely loved it. How has running a frontier lab been? The Meta Muse Spark level is the Opus level. What's coming down the pipe? And I think you're increasingly looking at open source, which this audience really loves.

**Alexandr Wang:** I've been at Meta for about a year now, and it's been quite a year. Meta has talked publicly about how Llama 4 wasn't on the trajectory needed for Meta, so I got in there and we did a zero-based build of how to build an entire frontier lab, in some ways from scratch, obviously using a lot of what we had, and move as quickly as possible.

Within nine months of that moment, we launched Muse Spark 1. Two months later, we launched Muse Image and Muse Spark 1.1.

The first thing that really struck me is that talent density was incredibly important. That was the core thing to bet on. Talent density compounds naturally: the more talented people you have, the more of the most talented people want to join you.

Frontier AI work is research. It is scientific work. We're exploring what you can do with these models, how you can push these models, and what the reaches of what can be accomplished with these models are. That requires a totally different mindset and operating model from what existed for internet companies or internet products.

There's much more experimentation, science, and scaling. Everything is ultimately about how you develop a lab, an operating model, and a system that can compound with all of the exponential growth that will happen in the ecosystem: exponential growth in capabilities, compute, adoption, and usage.

We're on a very steep exponent across maybe every dimension of the ecosystem. It's important to develop an organism—that's how I think about the lab—that's able to grow with that.

It's been very exciting, and we're going to be shipping a lot more. We just launched Muse Spark 1.1, which was a great model. We're going to continue to have updates on the Muse Spark line. We're also going to have bigger models on the way that I think will be much more competitive with even the very best models that are out there today.

We're going to be launching a harness soon. We've been working on a harness to help empower all the developers and agentic developers out there. We're also working on open-source models.

We believe in a decentralized world of AI capability, progress, and development. We want to empower the broader ecosystem and everyone in the world to be able to build and develop using this technology. We want to empower the ecosystem and developers as much as humanly possible.

**Host:** When I was using Muse Spark with my OpenClaw, it became clear that it was as good as Opus, especially for that sort of agentic flow with skill files, but it was eight times cheaper. [laughter]

**Alexandr Wang:** We don't believe in a world where these models are so expensive that they get rationed only for the wealthiest developers and companies. It's important for everyone to be able to use the technology and build whatever they want to build with it.

The best AI products haven't even been developed yet. If you look at the AI ecosystem and everything that's happened, every wave is ten times bigger than the past wave.

When I started Scale, the first wave was maybe self-driving cars. Self-driving cars are really awesome and really cool, but they pale in comparison to large language models and chatbots. Chatbots became something probably ten times bigger than self-driving cars.

Then there were coding agents, which came a few years later. Coding agents are probably ten times bigger than chatbots. I think we're just on this steep curve. We're going to keep seeing these new modalities, form factors, and developments of the AI paradigm, each dramatically bigger than the last.

Our point of view is: let's unleash the ecosystem, explore, and build the future of the world together.

## Models, Harnesses, and the Future

**Host:** What's the best way to take advantage of the coding model from Muse Spark?

**Alexandr Wang:** Today, the easiest way is to use OpenCode. We have onboarding on the website. Soon we'll have a harness of our own.

Ultimately, I think we want great models that plug into all of the available harnesses and empower as much combinatorial innovation in the ecosystem as possible.

**Host:** I know the harness is still under wraps, but can you tease us? I still use OpenClaw and Hermes Agent. These things are like Ferraris that break down on the side of the road all the time. Is this a Ferrari that won't break down?

**Alexandr Wang:** Hopefully it doesn't break down. We're really focused on speed. For anyone who uses these tools, speed is probably one of the most critical things.

Reliability is also important, as you mentioned. We want to be extremely reliable, very extensible, and able to scale to as complex and interesting a multi-agent setup as you want to have.

I think so much innovation will occur even above the harness—in how to orchestrate and set up loops, and develop very complex ecosystems of these agents working together. We want to be really extensible, and ultimately we want to empower people to harness this technology.

**Host:** So the harness—

**Alexandr Wang:** Oh, [laughter] no pun actually intended. [laughter]

These models are already incredibly powerful. They should be powerful enough to fuel many points of expansion of GDP growth. I think it's up to smart people with vision and ambition to make all that happen.

## What Will Be Obvious in Hindsight?

**Host:** When you look back on the decade, what do you think they'll say was obvious in hindsight about AI that people are missing in real time right now?

So much of the debate these days is about how good the models are actually getting, whether they can bridge this issue, when we're going to get superintelligence—is it two years or five years?—and whether we're going to hit a wall.

**Alexandr Wang:** So much of that debate is, in some ways, a little bit of a waste of time. It's inevitable that we're going to have very powerful models. Rather than arguing about exactly when it was going to happen, we'll look back and say that arguing about the timing was shortsighted.

The reality is that we, as an entire human civilization, are on this incredible exponential. You cannot look at the progress of AI over the past decade and not be totally awestruck by how far it has come.

A decade ago, the best AI models could recognize cats in YouTube videos. Now we're talking to a digital god that can— I mean, we've all seen some of the hacks and things these systems are capable of. You can't help but be awestruck. These models are going to become more and more powerful, and this trend will continue.

Looking back a decade from now, it will be obvious that intelligence became abundant and agency became abundant. The current trends are just going to keep continuing.

For most of human history, groups of smart people getting together toward a shared goal were the bottleneck of progress. The United States of America was an example of this: a smart group of very smart people getting together and having a vision for the future that they wanted to enact.

That's the story of nearly every company in America, and the story of every YC company. That's going to change. All of a sudden, the scarce resource isn't going to be intelligence or agency. I really think it's going to be vision and ambition.

Do you have a clear view of what you want the world to look like in the future? What is the one way in which you want to put your finger on the scale for how the future develops, and how the world will look in five to ten years so that it does not look like today? Do you have the ambition and drive to go through all the crap to make that happen?

AI will make that easier. Agents and AI make it maybe ten or a hundred times easier than it was a decade ago. But the flip side is that you can dream bigger.

There are so many things that need to evolve for us to fully embrace this technology. The world is barely even ready for it today. As builders, we have a responsibility to prepare the world.

We have to help enterprises and governments adapt to this new technology. We have to help figure out how to secure the world from a biosecurity or cybersecurity perspective. We have to figure out how we're going to manage all the risks that come with this new technology.

On the flip side, it's also a time of unprecedented opportunity for humans. We can develop new sciences. We can solve problems in health and biology that have been unsolved forever. We can build new businesses that couldn't have been imagined before. There are new creative opportunities that couldn't have existed before.

It's an incredible cradle of opportunity and risk, and I don't think there's a better time to be someone who is a builder and has a strong view of how the world should change.

## How Should People Prepare?

**Host:** Do you think the path has changed? I saw that at Stanford, the number of computer science majors actually dropped by some double-digit percentage. People are worried, which seems insane to me. You still need those skills to create agents that are that good—maybe that won't be true, I'm not really sure.

What would you say to people in this audience? This is a real question people are facing. Should they become more “word cell” and less “shape rotator”? What's the move? Has that changed the kind of people you're looking to hire, and how you manage your teams at Meta?

**Alexandr Wang:** Systematic and rigorous thinking are still incredibly important. The abstraction layer just keeps changing. I didn't used to believe this is how it was going to play out, but it really has.

When I started a company, we wrote code. [laughter] Now, I'm sure nobody here writes code anymore. That's ridiculous. [laughter]

Now it's about how you orchestrate agents together. Then it's about how you develop organizations of agents. How do you get a million agents to work together well? Then it will be how you get a trillion agents to work together well.

There will be a continued need to figure out how to structure workflows at the abstraction layer where we're operating. That form of rigorous, systematic thinking will never go out of style.

Traditionally, when I was starting companies, you would start by writing code, then have organizations of humans, and figure out how to organize those humans. That requires systems thinking. Now maybe it's much closer to: first you orchestrate the agent, then you figure out how to orchestrate armies of agents.

I think it's definitely a mistake to go all in on “word cell.” You need to “shape rotate.” But what's much more necessary in the future is a deeper compass and philosophical view on how the world should develop.

There are many lessons from human history about how civilizations go through these periods. Humanity will change more in the next decade than it has in the past 100 years, probably. The imperative for us to have positive visions for that, and coherent articulations of how it should develop, is more important than ever.

## Agentic Loops

**Host:** Let's get a little more concrete. Are there applications of AI that you're seeing among your friends or internally at Meta that you can talk about—obvious near-term applications that people haven't figured out yet? Give us some alpha. [laughter]

**Alexandr Wang:** There's still astronomical opportunity in agentic looping and figuring out how to develop systems that enable you to spend a thousand times more, or a million times more, on tokens to drive an outcome in a continuous feedback loop.

Companies are large-scale feedback loops where humans are operating each of the edges. They get customers, figure out how to make those customers happier, and if customers are happier, they spend more. If they spend more, the company can hire more people who can figure out how to get more customers and make those customers happier.

That's the feedback loop of every startup or business. Within it, there are micro-feedback loops. Developing agentic systems that can operate and optimize these feedback loops offers huge amounts of alpha.

We've seen cases internally at Meta where, if you develop the right agentic loop and have the right evaluation or metric for the agents to optimize, you can have a swarm of agents accomplish more than a team of 100 engineers, very handily and very easily.

Figuring out what the world looks like with lots of these agentic coordination problems is one of the most interesting problems today.

**Host:** Mechanically speaking, is it markdown files, cron jobs, and pointing the agent at enough data so it can figure something out that maybe isn't in distribution?

**Alexandr Wang:** Mechanically, figuring out what the metric is—and then it comes down to skills, markdown files, cron jobs, and `/goal`. [laughter]

It's always funny how mundane everything is once you really dig into it. It's not magic.

**Host:** Some people put a lot of magic around it. There are LinkedIn threads about magical stuff.

**Alexandr Wang:** Advice: ignore LinkedIn. LinkedIn is where you get customers. [laughter]

## A Message to His Younger Self

**Host:** I like to end on this. If you could send a telegram to the 18-year-old version of yourself, what would you say to that person right now?

**Alexandr Wang:** It really boils down to developing your own internal compass for how you think the future will develop, and having strong conviction in it.

You will get inundated with noise and people telling you what to do. It can be very confusing and very hard. Especially when you're young and don't have experience, it can feel difficult to have true conviction in what you believe and what you want to do.

But I think that's the most important thing. It took a deep conviction in what we were building to weather the storms of many years of chaos in the market, the industry, and the people around us.

The other piece of advice is to try to identify what the exponential is in the world that has both the steepest curve and will go the longest.

Many decades ago, that curve was Moore's law, and that was clearly the right thing to invest in at the time. I think right now it's AI progress, but there will be more of these very steep curves in the future.

It's fine if these curves start from a boring place, or if the starting point doesn't seem that interesting. When we started Scale, we had cat detectors in YouTube videos. It's hard to say that was the most important technology of our time, but it was on this unbelievable exponential.

I have one last thing to say.
